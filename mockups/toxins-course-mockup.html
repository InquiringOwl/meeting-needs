TIER 2 · ROOTS

Toxins

Bodies (ours, our companions’, our plants’ and the soil’s) need to not be quietly worn down by what’s in the pan, the can, the dust, the ground and the air next door. Seven units that start with how exposure works, then move outward from the kitchen to the neighborhood. Every section ends with a How to fix, free steps first.

One by oneMost fixes are swaps

Plastics, cleaners, pans, fragrance: knock them out one at a time, at your pace and budget. Each swap you add goes on My list in the order you choose.

Test onceSome things are a single batch

Yard soil and well water are cheapest and clearest tested once, all at the same time, with a plan. Unit 5 has a planner that builds one lab order.

HabitSome are small daily moves

Shoes off, wet dusting, a fan when cooking. Free, and they stack.

TogetherSome are bigger than a household

A freeway, a refinery, a spray field. These move with neighbors, maps and a voice in the room.

Fun fact

When families in one study swapped to fresh food with no cans or plastic packaging for just three days, the BPA in their urine dropped by about two-thirds. Some exposures leave the body that fast, which is part of why one-by-one swaps feel good: the body answers quickly.

Rudel et al., Environmental Health Perspectives 2011. Laws mentioned are California’s; the chemistry is the same everywhere.

    How exposures work

    A few ideas make every later unit easier: how much, by which door, at what age, and for how long it stays. With these, a scary headline turns into a question you can answer: does this reach me, and what’s the easiest link to break?

    1.1

    Dose, route and timing

    Three questions sit under every exposure. How much (the dose) and how often. Which door it uses: breathing it, eating or drinking it, or through skin. And when in life: pregnancy, babies and young children take in more per pound of body, put hands and toys in their mouths, and are still building organs, so the same dose lands harder.

    DoorCommon examplesEasiest link to break
    BreathingCooking smoke, gas stove fumes, sprays, dust, wildfire smoke, freeway airVentilation, filters, fewer sprays
    Eating & drinkingPlastic touching hot or fatty food, can linings, residues, tap water, house dust on handsSwap the container, wash produce, filter water, wash hands
    SkinLotions, fragrance, cleaners, treated fabrics, soilGloves, simpler products, rinse off

    One wrinkle: some chemicals that mimic hormones (endocrine disruptors) don’t follow “a little is always fine.” Small amounts at sensitive times can matter, which is part of why this course leans on reducing many small exposures rather than chasing one big one.

    How to fixHabitFree
    1. Free · todayPick one room and name the doors: what’s breathed, eaten and touched there.
    2. FreeIf someone is pregnant or there’s a baby or toddler, start with what they touch and mouth: floors, dust, cups, bottles.
    3. FreeWash hands before eating, especially kids after floor and yard play. It cuts dust-borne lead, flame retardants and pesticides at once.

    US EPA, Child-Specific Exposure Factors Handbook; Endocrine Society scientific statement on endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDC-2, 2015).

    1.2

    Source, path, body

    Every exposure is a chain: a source (a pan, a can, a factory), a path (heat, dust, air, water, hands) and a body it reaches. Breaking any one link works. Safety engineers rank the ways to break it, and the order is useful at home too:

    1 · Remove it

    Don’t bring it in, or let it go. Most powerful, often free.

    2 · Swap it

    Glass for plastic, cast iron for a scratched nonstick pan.

    3 · Block the path

    A range hood, a filter, a doormat, a raised bed with clean soil.

    4 · Change the habit

    Shoes off, food cooled before it goes in plastic.

    5 · Protect the body

    Gloves, a mask. Useful, but the last line, not the first.

    Microwaving leftovers in a plastic tub: the source is the tub, the path is heat, the body is whoever eats. Moving the food to a plate before heating removes the path for free.

    How to fixHabitFree
    1. Free · todayFor one worry, write the chain: source → path → body.
    2. FreeCircle the link that’s cheapest to break. Often it’s the path (heat, dust, air), not the thing itself.
    3. FreeOnly buy something when removing, moving or changing a habit won’t do it.
    Uses the same idea as the Water lens’s source-to-tap chain.

    NIOSH, Hierarchy of Controls.

    1.3

    Gone in days, here for years

    Some chemicals pass through the body in hours or days. Others stay for years. This changes what a fix can do, and how soon.

    ChemicalRoughly how long half of it staysWhat that means
    BPA, many phthalatesHours to a daySwaps show up in the body within days.
    GlyphosateHours to daysDiet changes show up within a week.
    LeadAbout a month in blood; decades in boneStopping new exposure matters most, early.
    PFOA, PFOS (PFAS)About 2 to 5 yearsFixes are slow to show; preventing new exposure, especially in water, is the lever.

    Green: leaves quickly · Amber: mixed · Red: stays for years.

    How to fixHabitFree
    1. FreeFor quick-leaving chemicals, small daily swaps pay off fast. Start there for momentum.
    2. FreeFor long-staying ones (PFAS, lead), look for the biggest single source, usually water or soil, and deal with it once.
    3. AskWith likely high PFAS exposure, a clinician can order a PFAS blood test (US National Academies guidance, 2022).
    Links to Water → Testing.

    ATSDR toxicological profiles (lead, PFAS); Li et al. 2018 on PFAS half-lives; National Academies, Guidance on PFAS Exposure, Testing, and Clinical Follow-Up (2022).

    1.4

    Every body, not just ours

    The house holds more than people. Some kin are far more sensitive than we are, and their reactions can be early signals for everyone.

    Birds Air

    Overheated nonstick pans give off fumes that can kill a bird in minutes. Birds breathe so efficiently that fumes hit them first.

    Cats Skin

    Permethrin, common in dog flea treatments, can poison a cat, sometimes fatally. Cats also can’t break down many essential oils well.

    Dogs Water

    Toxic algae in still water can kill a dog within hours of a swim or a drink.

    Plants Roots

    Microplastics and PFAS reach roots and, for some crops, leaves and fruit. Herbicide drift scorches non-target plants.

    Soil life Ground

    Worms, fungi and microbes take the brunt of what lands on the ground, and they’re the ones who rebuild it.

    When someone small is sneezing, itching, listless or drooping, it’s a message about a need. Ask what changed in the air, water, food or soil before anything else.

    How to fixHabitFree
    1. Free · todaySave the ASPCA Animal Poison Control number (888-426-4435, a fee may apply) in your phone.
    2. FreeKeep birds out of the kitchen while cooking, and check any flea treatment is labeled for that species.
    3. FreeRead later units with the most sensitive one in the house in mind.

    ASPCA Animal Poison Control; Merck Veterinary Manual (PTFE toxicosis in birds; permethrin toxicosis in cats); CDC, Harmful Algal Blooms and animals.

    Unit 2

    Plastics

    Including PFAS

    Plastic is useful and everywhere, and it isn’t one thing: it’s a polymer plus additives that soften it, harden it, color it or make it slick. Some of those additives move into food and dust, and the plastic itself sheds tiny pieces. The good news: food contact is the main door, and it’s one we control a lot of.

    One by one Swap one food-contact item at a time, starting with whatever meets heat.
    • 2.1What’s in it
    • 2.2Heat, fat, acid
    • 2.3Cans & lids
    • 2.4PFAS
    • 2.5Soil & plants
    2.1

    What’s in plastic

    • Bisphenols (BPA, and replacements BPS and BPF) harden polycarbonate and epoxy can linings, and coat many paper receipts. They act like estrogen in the body.
    • Phthalates soften vinyl (PVC) and carry fragrance. Linked to changes in male reproductive development and to asthma.
    • Micro- and nanoplastics: pieces from shedding and wear. Found in human blood, placentas and brain tissue; in one 2024 study, people with plastic in their artery plaque had higher rates of heart attack, stroke and death. Cause and effect is still being studied.
    People

    Hormone signaling, reproduction, metabolism; plastics research is fast-moving and mostly associations so far.

    Animals

    Wild animals eat plastic outright; companion animals share our dust and bowls.

    Plants & soil

    See 2.5: plastic changes how soil holds water and air, and tiny pieces enter some roots.

    Recycling codes, roughly: 3 (PVC) and 6 (polystyrene) and 7 (“other,” often polycarbonate) are the ones to retire first from food use; 2, 4, 5 shed fewer known additives, but still shed particles with heat and wear.

    How to fixOne by oneFree → $
    1. Free · todaySay no thanks to paper receipts, or hold them briefly and wash hands after.
    2. FreeRetire scratched, cloudy or #3/#6/#7 food containers to garage storage (screws, crayons).
    3. Free → $Collect glass jars from food you already buy; they replace most tubs at no cost.

    Endocrine Society EDC-2 (2015); Leslie et al., Environment International 2022 (blood); Marfella et al., NEJM 2024 (carotid plaque); Nihart et al., Nature Medicine 2025 (brain tissue).

    2.2

    Heat, fat and acid

    Plastic gives up more into food when it’s hot, when the food is fatty (oil, nut butters, coconut milk) or acidic (tomato, citrus, vinegar), when it’s old and scratched, and the longer they touch. In one lab study, microwaving plastic containers released millions of microplastic and billions of nanoplastic pieces per square centimeter in three minutes.

    • Hot drinks through plastic lids or plastic tea bags.
    • Hot food poured into plastic tubs, or warmed in them.
    • Dishwashers: heat plus detergent wears plastic fast.
    • Plastic cutting boards shed into food as they’re scored.
    How to fixOne by oneFree first
    1. Free · todayReheat on a plate or in a pot, never in plastic. Cool food before it goes into any plastic.
    2. FreeHand-wash any plastic you keep; skip the dishwasher for it.
    3. Low costLoose-leaf tea with a metal strainer; a wooden cutting board; glass or steel for leftovers.
    BabiesBottles, formula and teethersFor you
    • BPA has been out of US baby bottles since 2012, but plastic bottles still shed microplastics, more with hot water. Make formula in a glass or steel container and pour it in once it’s cooled to drinking warmth.
    • Glass bottles with silicone sleeves don’t shed plastic and survive a lot.
    • Soft vinyl toys and teethers were where phthalates were restricted first (US 2008); older hand-me-downs may predate that.
    Little controlWhen the food comes in plastic anywayFor you

    Shelter meals, pantry boxes and takeout often come in plastic, and that’s fine to accept. What still helps: let hot food cool a little before eating from the container, and move it to a mug or bowl when one is around.

    Hussain et al., Environmental Science & Technology 2023; Hernandez et al., ES&T 2019 (plastic tea bags); Li et al., Nature Food 2020 (infant bottles); FDA, BPA in baby bottles (2012); CPSIA 2008.

    2.3

    Cans and jar lids

    Metal cans are lined with a thin plastic coat so food doesn’t touch metal. For decades that lining was BPA-based epoxy. In one study, eating one serving of canned soup a day for five days raised people’s urine BPA more than ten-fold compared to fresh soup. Most US cans now use “BPA-NI” (non-intent) linings: acrylic, polyester, or sometimes vinyl or BPS-based coatings, which aren’t always better studied. Jar lids carry a plastic seal too.

    Canned foodPicks up more?Swap
    Tomatoes, tomato pasteYes: acidic, often heat-filledJarred or boxed (aseptic carton) tomatoes, or fresh in season
    Coconut milkYes: fattyCarton or creamed coconut block
    SoupsOftenHomemade from dried beans and grains (Food → Rehydrate)
    Beans, chickpeasSomeDried beans: cheaper too. Or jarred.
    Fruit in syrupSomeFrozen fruit, jars

    BPA is on California’s Proposition 65 list for reproductive harm (since 2015), so some cans carry a warning. The EU banned BPA in food-contact materials in late 2024, with a phase-in through 2026.

    How to fixOne by oneSaves money
    1. Free · this weekSwap canned beans for a weekly pot of dried beans. It costs less, too.
    2. Same costSwitch canned tomatoes and coconut milk to glass or carton next time they run out.
    3. FreeWhen you do use cans, move leftovers out of the can the same day.

    Food → Rehydrate (soaking beans) · Food → Store (glass jars)

    Carwile et al., JAMA 2011; OEHHA Prop 65 listing (BPA, 2015); Commission Regulation (EU) 2024/3190; Breast Cancer Prevention Partners, Kicking the Can? (can lining survey).

    2.4

    PFAS, the forever chemicals

    PFAS are thousands of related chemicals that make things slick, stain-proof and water-proof. Their carbon–fluorine bond is one of the strongest in chemistry, so they barely break down: in water, soil, or bodies. Almost everyone in the US has some in their blood.

    • Where they show up: drinking water near airports, military bases and factories; nonstick coatings; grease-proof food wrappers and bowls; stain- and water-resistant carpet, couches, rain gear; some dental floss and cosmetics; firefighting foam.
    • What they’re linked to: higher cholesterol, weaker vaccine response in children, lower birth weight, thyroid disease, and kidney and testicular cancer. The WHO’s cancer agency classed PFOA as carcinogenic to humans in 2023.

    Plant-fiber food packaging (molded bowls, wrappers) can’t have added PFAS since 2023 (AB 1200), cookware has to disclose PFAS, and new textiles have been PFAS-restricted since 2025 (AB 1817). A wider cookware ban (SB 682) was vetoed in 2025. Federally, EPA set 4-part-per-trillion limits for PFOA and PFOS in tap water in 2024; in 2026 it proposed dropping the other PFAS limits and pushing compliance to 2031.

    How to fixTest oncethen one by oneFree → $$
    1. Free · firstWater first: it’s usually the largest source. Look up your water system’s PFAS results (Water → Testing), or test a well once.
    2. $ → $$If PFAS are present: a reverse osmosis or certified activated-carbon filter (NSF/ANSI 53 or 58 for PFOA/PFOS).
    3. One by oneThen: nonstick pans (3.4), grease-proof takeout, stain-guard sprays, “water-resistant” anything, when each wears out.
    Water lens holds the filter details.
    Well waterTesting a private well for PFASFor you

    PFAS tests are pricier than a basic panel (often a few hundred dollars), so bundle it with the rest of your well test in one sample run. California’s State Water Board keeps a map of known PFAS sites; nearby airports, bases and landfills raise the odds.

    Water → Testing

    ATSDR, PFAS health effects; IARC Monographs vol. 135 (2023); US EPA PFAS NPDWR (2024) and 2026 proposed revisions; California AB 1200 (2021), AB 1817 (2022).

    2.5

    Plastic in soil and plants

    Soil is now one of the largest reservoirs of microplastic, more than oceans by some estimates. It arrives on plastic mulch, synthetic fibers in compost and sewage sludge (biosolids), tire dust from roads, and old weed fabric breaking down.

    Soil

    Changes how soil holds water and air; can slow worms and shift microbe communities.

    Plants

    In lab studies, crops like lettuce and wheat took nanoplastic in through roots and moved some to leaves. PFAS travel the same road, short-chain ones most into leaves and fruit.

    People

    What the plant takes up, we eat, though rinsing and peeling don’t remove what’s inside.

    Worms and soil fungi are the ones rebuilding soil. Keeping plastic out of the beds is a gift to them first.

    How to fixOne by oneFree → $
    1. FreePull old landscape fabric and plastic mulch as you go; use straw, leaves or cardboard (tape off) instead.
    2. FreePeel fruit stickers before composting; keep “compostable” plastics out unless you know your pile gets hot.
    3. Free → $Skip compost or fertilizer made from biosolids (“sewage sludge”) for food beds; see 5.3.

    Soil → Potting mix · Gardening lens

    Li et al., Nature Sustainability 2020 (crop uptake); de Souza Machado et al., Global Change Biology 2018; Lesmeister et al. 2021 on PFAS in plants.

    In the house

    Cleaners, fragrance, dust, pans and body care

    Indoor air is often more polluted than the air outside, mostly from what we bring in and use. This is the unit built for knocking things out one at a time: each bottle, pan or couch gets swapped when it runs out or wears out, so it costs little and nothing goes to waste.

    One by one Replace each thing when it runs out. Habit Dust and air habits are free from day one.

    3.1

    Cleaning products

    Most cleaning can be done with water, soap, baking soda, vinegar and a scrub brush. Stronger products earn their place for specific jobs (disinfecting after raw-food prep or illness, mold), not for everyday wiping. Sprays put more into the air we breathe than pour-and-wipe.

    • Quats (“benzalkonium chloride,” many disinfecting wipes) are linked to asthma in cleaning workers.
    • Bleach works well, but never with ammonia (toxic chloramine gas) or acids like vinegar (chlorine gas).
    • Oven and drain cleaners are the most caustic in most homes.

    Never mix cleaners. Bleach + ammonia, or bleach + vinegar or acid toilet cleaners, makes gases that injure lungs.

    How to fixOne by oneSaves money
    1. Free · todayGather every cleaner in one place. Note which jobs each is for; retire duplicates to hazardous-waste drop-off.
    2. As each runs outReplace it with a basic: castile or dish soap, baking soda, vinegar, washing soda. Pour onto a cloth instead of spraying.
    3. Keep oneOne real disinfectant for when it’s needed, used with a window open.

    Cleaning lens (recipes for each job) · Air lens (ventilation)

    US EPA, Indoor Air Quality: household products; Dumas et al., JAMA Network Open 2019 (disinfectants and COPD in nurses); Washington State Dept. of Health, chemical mixing hazards.

    3.2

    Fragrance

    “Fragrance” on a label can mean dozens of undisclosed ingredients, sometimes including phthalates that make scent last. Air fresheners, plug-ins, scented candles, dryer sheets and laundry “boosters” are among the biggest indoor sources of volatile chemicals, and some react with ozone to make formaldehyde.

    Cats lack some liver enzymes needed to clear many essential oils; diffusers can make them and birds sick. Unscented is kindest for everyone with a nose.

    Since 2021–2022, California requires cleaning products and cosmetics to list fragrance allergens on the label (SB 312 and the Cleaning Product Right to Know Act).

    How to fixOne by oneFree
    1. Free · todayUnplug plug-in fresheners. Open a window or run a fan for 10 minutes instead.
    2. FreeFind the smell’s source (trash, damp, drain) and meet that need; a smell is a signal.
    3. As it runs outLaundry soap: fragrance-free. Dryer sheets: wool balls or nothing.

    Steinemann, Air Quality, Atmosphere & Health 2018; California Cleaning Product Right to Know Act (SB 258, 2017); Cosmetic Fragrance and Flavor Ingredient Right to Know Act (SB 312, 2020).

    3.3

    Dust and flame retardants

    House dust is where many indoor chemicals end up: flame retardants from couches and electronics, PFAS from carpet treatments, phthalates, lead tracked in from soil, and pesticide residues. Toddlers swallow the most, from crawling and hands-in-mouth.

    California’s old furniture rule (TB 117) pushed flame retardants into US couches for decades. Since 2014 the rule can be met without them, and furniture labels say whether flame retardants were added. Look for “contains NO added flame retardant chemicals.”

    How to fixHabitFree → $$
    1. Free · todayShoes off at the door, and a doormat outside. A large share of house dust is tracked in on shoes.
    2. FreeWet-dust and damp-mop weekly instead of dry sweeping, which lifts dust back into the air.
    3. $$ · laterA HEPA vacuum; and when an old foam couch tears, replace or recover it with a no-flame-retardant label.
    Built before 1978Lead paint in dustFor you

    Lead paint was banned for homes in 1978. When older paint chips or rubs (window sills and friction surfaces most), it becomes dust. Free lead dust wipe kits are offered by some county health departments; a home lead inspection is the bigger step. Sanding or scraping old paint needs lead-safe methods.

    Renters: landlords in California can’t create lead hazards, and you can report deteriorating paint to local code enforcement.

    Mitro et al., Environmental Science & Technology 2016 (dust meta-analysis); California TB 117-2013 and SB 1019 (labels); US EPA, Lead in homes.

    3.4

    Pots and pans

    Nonstick (PTFE, often sold as Teflon) is stable at normal heat, but starts breaking down around 500°F (260°C), a temperature an empty pan on high heat can reach in a few minutes. The fumes can cause flu-like “polymer fume fever” in people and can kill birds. Scratched coatings flake into food.

    MaterialNotesCost
    Cast ironLasts generations; naturally slick when seasoned. Long-simmered tomato adds a little iron.Often free or cheap used
    Carbon steelLighter cast iron; great for tofu scrambles, stir-fries, crepes.$
    Stainless steelInert, dishwasher-safe. Food sticks less once the pan is preheated.$ used, $$ new
    Glass, enameled ironInert. Check old enamel and imported ceramics for lead glaze.$–$$
    “Ceramic” nonstickPFAS-free sol-gel coating; nonstick wears off in a year or two.$
    How to fixOne by oneFree → $
    1. Free · todayUntil it’s replaced: never preheat nonstick empty, keep it on medium, run the hood, birds out of the kitchen.
    2. Free → $Retire scratched nonstick first. Thrift stores and Buy Nothing groups are full of cast iron.
    3. FreeWooden or steel utensils, which also keep any pan’s surface longer.

    Food → Cook (pots, pans and stoves) · Air lens (gas stoves)

    ATSDR PFAS; Merck Veterinary Manual (PTFE toxicosis); FDA, lead in imported ceramicware.

    3.5

    Body care

    Lotions, shampoos and makeup stay on skin for hours. The ingredients most often flagged: fragrance (3.2), some preservatives like formaldehyde releasers, PFAS in some long-wear makeup and floss, and phthalates. Fewer products, with shorter ingredient lists, is the simplest path.

    California’s Toxic-Free Cosmetics Act bans 24 ingredients (including formaldehyde, mercury and certain phthalates) from cosmetics sold in the state since 2025, and a separate law (AB 2771) bars added PFAS from cosmetics the same year.

    How to fixOne by oneSaves money
    1. FreeNotice which products you’d honestly miss. Let the rest run out and not return.
    2. As each runs outChoose fragrance-free; short ingredient lists; plain oils (olive, coconut, jojoba) for skin.
    3. FreeKids need very little: water, a gentle soap, sunscreen.

    California Toxic-Free Cosmetics Act (AB 2762, 2020); Whitehead et al., ES&T Letters 2021 (PFAS in cosmetics).

    Unit 4

    Pesticides

    Glyphosate and kin

    “Pesticide” covers herbicides (for plants), insecticides (insects), fungicides (fungi) and rodenticides (rodents). Each is designed to harm a living thing, and few stop exactly at the one intended. In Kinship terms, the “pest” is a neighbor whose needs our home or garden is meeting by accident; change what’s offered and most move on.

    One by one food and yard. Together for drift from fields and parks.

    How pesticides work

    4.2

    Glyphosate

    Glyphosate (Roundup and many store brands) is the most-used herbicide in the world. It blocks an enzyme plants, fungi and many bacteria need to build certain amino acids (the shikimate pathway). Animals don’t have that pathway, which is why it was long called safe for us, but many of our gut bacteria do.

    People

    The WHO’s cancer agency classed it “probably carcinogenic” (2015), mainly over non-Hodgkin lymphoma; US EPA says it isn’t likely carcinogenic at expected exposures. The disagreement is real and ongoing.

    Bees

    Lab studies found it disrupts honey bee gut bacteria, leaving bees more vulnerable to infection.

    Plants

    Kills almost any green plant it touches, including milkweed, monarch butterflies’ only host. Drift damages gardens nearby.

    Soil

    Binds to soil and can shift microbe communities; breakdown time varies widely with soil and climate.

    On food: besides weed-killing, glyphosate is sprayed on some crops right before harvest to dry them down evenly, which leaves higher residues on oats, wheat, chickpeas, lentils and other dry beans. Certified organic rules don’t allow it.

    How to fixOne by one$ for a few staples
    1. Highest returnIf you buy organic for only a few things, make them oats, wheat flour, chickpeas and lentils: daily staples with the most pre-harvest spray.
    2. FreeNo glyphosate in your own yard: pull, mulch, smother with cardboard, or boiling water on cracks in paving.
    3. BulkOrganic staples in bulk with neighbors (Food → Gather) costs far less than small packages.

    In one study, families who switched to an all-organic diet saw urine glyphosate drop about 70% in under a week.

    IARC Monographs vol. 112 (2015); US EPA glyphosate interim decision; Motta et al., PNAS 2018 (bee microbiota); Fagan et al., Environmental Research 2020; USDA National Organic Program.

    4.3

    On our food

    Eating more fruit and vegetables is good for nearly everyone, residues and all. The aim isn’t eating fewer plants; it’s choosing where organic matters most and washing well.

    • Rinse under running water and rub, which removes more than soaking in still water. Produce washes don’t beat water.
    • A baking-soda soak (about 1 tsp per 2 cups water, 12–15 minutes, then rinse) removed more surface residue from apples than water alone in one study.
    • Peel where it makes sense; some pesticides are systemic, inside the plant, and no wash removes them.
    • EWG’s yearly “Dirty Dozen” and “Clean Fifteen” lists rank produce by residue: a handy shortcut for which to buy organic.
    How to fixHabitFree → $
    1. Free · todayRinse and rub everything under running water, even “pre-washed” bags and peeled things (the knife carries residue in).
    2. FreeBaking-soda soak for apples, grapes and berries.
    3. $Buy organic for the top few of the Dirty Dozen your household eats most; conventional is fine for the Clean Fifteen.

    Food → Cleanse (washing produce)

    4.5

    Drift from next door

    Spray doesn’t stay where it lands. Fine droplets and vapors drift with wind and heat, sometimes for miles, into gardens, schools and homes. People living near farm fields have measurably more pesticide in house dust.

    California’s statewide SprayDays system (from 2025) posts notices of restricted farm pesticide applications in advance; county agricultural commissioners take drift complaints, and schools have a quarter-mile buffer for many applications.

    5.2

    Test once, in one batch

    One lab order covering every zone, sampled the same day, gives you a map you can garden by for years. The steps:

    1. Sketch the yard and draw zones by use and history: each food bed, the play area, the drip line, the street edge, where chickens or dogs dig.
    2. For each zone, take a composite sample: 5 to 10 small scoops from across the zone, 0–6 inches deep (0–2 inches for play areas), mixed in a clean bucket. About a cup goes in a labeled bag.
    3. Order one panel for all of them: total lead at minimum; add arsenic for old orchard land; a full metals panel near industry or after fire. Ask for pH and organic matter in the same run: both change how much lead plants take up.
    4. Send them together. Keep the map with the results.

    5.3

    Potting mix and bagged soil

    A bag of potting mix feels clean, but it’s a living, dusty product with its own care points.

    • Living germs: Legionella longbeachae, which can cause Legionnaires’ disease, lives in some potting mixes and compost; cases are tracked in Australia and New Zealand, where bags carry warnings, and occur in the US too. Dust from a dry bag is how it’s breathed in.
    • Biosolids: some bagged composts and fertilizers are made from treated sewage sludge, which can carry PFAS and microplastics. It isn’t always obvious on the label.
    • Dust: perlite and vermiculite dust irritate lungs. Vermiculite from Libby, Montana was contaminated with asbestos (mine closed in 1990); modern horticultural vermiculite is lower risk but still dusty.
    • Wetting agents and synthetic fertilizer are added to many mixes; fine for plants, but worth knowing for food pots.
    • Peat comes from bogs that store huge amounts of carbon and take centuries to regrow.
    How to fixHabitOne by oneFree → $
    1. Free · every timeOpen bags outside, pointed away from your face. Wet the mix before handling; wash hands after.
    2. $For food pots, choose mixes that are certified organic or OMRI-listed: organic rules don’t allow sewage sludge.
    3. Free · over timeMake your own: home compost + coir or leaf mold + a little sand. Grows with the household.
    Pots onlyContainer gardensFor you

    Bagged soil is the whole garden here, which makes it the only soil “test” you need: what’s in the bag. A dust mask (N95) for bagging-up days is a kind gift to your lungs, especially for anyone with asthma or a weaker immune system.

    CDC, Legionella (potting soil and L. longbeachae); Whiley & Bentham, Emerging Infectious Diseases 2011; USDA National Organic Program (sewage sludge prohibited); US EPA, vermiculite.

    5.4

    Beds, wood and tires

    • Old pressure-treated wood (greenish, from before 2004) usually used CCA, which contains arsenic. It leaches into soil nearby. Newer treated wood uses copper compounds instead.
    • Railroad ties carry creosote; skip them for food beds.
    • Tires as planters leach zinc and other compounds as they weather. 6PPD-quinone from tire dust kills coho salmon in streams.
    • Painted salvage may have lead paint.
    How to fixOne by oneFree → $$
    1. FreeGrow food away from old treated-wood edges (a foot in), and include that bed in your soil batch.
    2. Free → $New beds: untreated cedar, redwood offcuts, stone, brick, or galvanized steel.
    3. FreeRetire tire planters to non-food flowers or recycling.

    US EPA, CCA treated wood phase-out (2003); Tian et al., Science 2021 (6PPD-quinone).

    5.5

    Living with what you find

    Most results fall in a range where gardening stays a joy with a few changes. Soil heals slowly, and the main move is covering it so it doesn’t become dust.

    • Cover bare soil with mulch, groundcover or grass: bare soil becomes house dust.
    • Keep pH near neutral (6.5–7) and organic matter high: compost binds lead and makes it less available to plants.
    • Fruiting crops (tomatoes, squash, beans) take up the least; leafy greens and roots the most.
    • Raised beds with a barrier (landscape cloth or cardboard) and new soil, for higher results.

    About sunflowers: they’re lovely, but they pull up very little lead. Plants that clean soil of lead in a garden’s lifetime haven’t panned out.

    How to fixHabitFree → $$
    1. FreeMulch every bare patch, most of all where kids play and dogs dig.
    2. FreeAdd compost every season; wash and peel root crops; rinse greens well.
    3. $$For high zones, a raised bed with new soil. Many cities share soil from municipal compost.

    US EPA, Reusing Potentially Contaminated Landscapes: Growing Gardens in Urban Soils; McBride et al. 2014 on lead uptake by vegetables.

    Unit 6

    Living toxins

    Molds, blooms and plants

    Some toxins are made by living things: molds growing on damp walls or old nuts, bacteria blooming in warm still water, plants protecting themselves from being eaten. They’re signals more than enemies. Each one is answering a need (water, warmth, food), and changing that is usually the fix.

    Habit Moisture and storage habits do most of the work. Testing for mold is rarely needed: if you see or smell it, fix it.

    6.1

    Mold in the house

    Mold needs water. Damp homes are linked with more coughing, wheezing and asthma, in children most. Mold spores are everywhere outdoors; indoors, the question is only whether something’s wet enough for them to grow.

    • Common spots: bathroom ceilings, window sills with condensation, behind furniture on outside walls, under sinks, closets, carpets that got wet.
    • A musty smell is mold you can’t see yet.
    • Indoor humidity between 30 and 50% keeps most mold from growing.
    How to fixHabitFree → $
    1. Free · firstFind the water: leak, condensation, a shower without a fan. Mold comes back until the water stops.
    2. FreeSmall areas (under about 10 sq ft): scrub with soap and water, dry fully. Porous things that stayed wet (ceiling tiles, carpet) usually need to go.
    3. $A $10 humidity meter; bathroom fan for 20 minutes after showers; furniture a few inches off outside walls.
    RentingWhen the leak isn’t yours to fixFor you

    Since 2016, visible mold that affects health counts as a substandard-housing condition in California (SB 655). Write to the landlord with photos and dates, and keep a copy. Local code enforcement can inspect if it isn’t fixed.

    Mold on food

    6.3

    Water that blooms

    Cyanobacteria (blue-green algae) bloom in warm, still, nutrient-rich water: ponds, lakes, slow rivers, even birdbaths and stock tanks. Some make toxins that harm the liver and nerves. Dogs are most at risk: they swim, drink and lick their fur, and can die within hours.

    • Look for pea-soup green, blue-green paint, scum, mats, or a foul smell.
    • Lawn and farm fertilizer runoff feeds blooms.
    How to fixHabitTogetherFree
    1. Free“When in doubt, stay out”: keep everyone, dogs first, out of scummy water; rinse a dog right away if they go in.
    2. FreeChange birdbath and animal water bowls outdoors every few days.
    3. TogetherReport blooms (with a photo) so others are warned; use less fertilizer near water.

    The State Water Board’s Harmful Algal Bloom portal (mywaterquality.ca.gov) maps reported blooms and takes reports.

    CDC, Harmful Algal Bloom-Associated Illness; California Water Boards, HAB portal.

    6.5

    Most microbes are kin

    The vast majority of molds, yeasts and bacteria are helpers: they make soil, compost, sourdough, miso and tempeh, and they train our immune systems. Children who grow up around soil, plants and animals tend to have fewer allergies. The aim of this unit is to dry out the few that harm, not to sterilize the house.

    How to fixHabitFree
    1. FreeSkip antibacterial soaps and sprays for everyday use; plain soap works and spares the rest.
    2. FreeGarden, compost and ferment together. Hands in soil (washed after) are part of the fun.

    7.2

    Roads, freeways and airports

    Traffic pollution (fine particles, ultrafine particles, nitrogen dioxide, tire and brake dust) is highest within about 500 feet of a freeway and falls off with distance. It’s linked to asthma, reduced lung growth in children, and heart disease. Small airports add lead: piston-engine planes still burn leaded fuel.

    The state air board has long advised not placing homes, schools and daycares within 500 feet of freeways. After a study found higher blood lead in children near Reid-Hillview airport, Santa Clara County stopped selling leaded aviation fuel at its airports in 2022. EPA found leaded avgas endangers health in 2023.

    7.3

    Factories, refineries and wells

    Refineries, chemical plants, metal shops, chrome platers, oil and gas wells and warehouses with heavy truck traffic release particles, benzene, metals and other compounds. Flaring and leaks spike exposure for hours; routine releases add up over years.

    California now bars new oil and gas wells within 3,200 feet of homes, schools and hospitals, and requires leak monitoring for existing ones nearby (SB 1137, in effect since 2024). Refineries must run fenceline monitors with public data.

    7.4

    After a fire

    When homes, cars and buildings burn, the ash holds metals (lead, arsenic), plastics’ breakdown products and more, unlike ash from a campfire. Smoke from a distant wildfire is mostly fine particles; ash at home is a soil and dust problem too. After some fires, melted plastic pipes have released benzene into water systems.

    How to fixHabitTest onceFree → $
    1. FreeDon’t dry-sweep or leaf-blow ash: mist it, then scoop or HEPA-vacuum. N95 and gloves while cleaning.
    2. FreeFollow your water system’s notices; flush or don’t drink until cleared.
    3. $ · onceAdd fire-affected areas to your soil batch (5.2), with a full metals panel. Ask the county about free screening.

    7.5

    Changing it together

    Pollution sources are permitted, which means there are hearings, comment periods and boards. Communities have closed plants, won buffers and got monitors installed by showing up with data and stories.

    • AB 617 (California, 2017) funds community air monitoring and emission-reduction plans in the most burdened neighborhoods, chosen with residents.
    • Community sensors (PurpleAir, borrowed from libraries in some areas) build a shared record.
    • Environmental justice groups already working nearby are often the fastest way in.

    Living near pollution is rarely a free choice; housing, history and money set where most of us live. Protecting your household and pushing on the source together are both care.
