Fun fact

This course leans on three kinds of knowing, checked against each other: old practice that kept people alive for thousands of years, international guidance (the WHO’s drinking-water guidelines and the Sphere humanitarian handbook), and what you can see, smell and test yourself. Legal limits come from politics as well as science, and they change, so we use them as one input, not the last word.

Laws mentioned are California’s. Water itself is the same everywhere.

Unit 1

Uses

Using water wisely

Not every job needs drinking water. Knowing what each use really needs is the base of every system later in this course, and the easiest way to need less.

  • 1.1Needs
  • 1.2Grades
  • 1.3Gray water
  • 1.4Use less

1.1

What we need it for

A day of water: drinking, cooking, brushing teeth, washing hands and bodies, dishes, laundry, flushing, cleaning, plants and animals. A body needs only about 2–3 liters a day to drink, more in heat. Humanitarian groups plan around 15 liters (about 4 gallons) per person a day for drinking, cooking and basic washing. A typical US home with taps uses many times that.

When every drop was carried, every drop had a job: washing water went to the garden, dishwater to the animals’ trough or the fruit trees.

Keep a one-day water diary: every time water runs, jot what it was for. Circle the jobs that didn’t need drinking-quality water.

1.2

Four grades of water

Match the water to the job, from cleanest to least clean.

Grade 1Drinking (potable)

Drinking, cooking, brushing teeth, baby formula, ice, washing produce eaten raw.

Grade 2Clean, boil if unsure

Washing hands and bodies, dishes, laundry. Rain or stored water fits here, boiled if anyone might swallow it.

Grade 3Gray water

Used once: shower, bath, bathroom sink, laundry. Good for flushing and watering non-edible plants.

Grade 4Black water

Toilet waste (and in many places, the kitchen sink). Goes to sewer, septic or a proper compost toilet.

A bowl in the sink while washing veggies catches grade 2 water for the houseplants.

1.3

Gray water

  • Use it within a day. Stored gray water starts to smell and grow germs.
  • Send it into soil or mulch, not onto leaves you eat or root crops.
  • Plant-friendly soaps (low salt, no boron or bleach) keep the soil happy.

A simple laundry-to-landscape system needs no permit if it follows the state plumbing code’s rules (no storage, no spraying, water stays on your land).

PortableGray water without changing any pipesFor you

A bucket in the shower while it warms up, then into the toilet tank or onto outdoor plants. A basin in the sink for hand-washing water. Nothing to install, nothing to ask permission for.

Long-term placeLaundry-to-landscape and branched drainsFor you

The washing machine’s drain hose feeds a pipe to mulch basins around trees. A branched drain does the same for showers. A 3-way valve lets you switch back to the sewer in rainy months.

Gardening · Composting & waste

OutageFlushing when the water is offFor you

Pour a bucket of gray water (about 1–2 gallons) quickly into the bowl to flush. If sewer lines may be broken after an earthquake, don’t flush: use a lined bucket toilet instead.

Composting & waste (Sewage) · Emergency preparedness

1.4

Small ways to use less

  • Toilet leak test: a few drops of food coloring in the tank. Color in the bowl after 10 minutes without flushing means a leak.
  • A faucet dripping once a second adds up to thousands of gallons a year.
  • Wash dishes in a basin, run full laundry loads, catch the cold water while the shower warms.
  • Water gardens early or late, at the roots, with mulch on top.

Do the toilet dye test with a child: they’re great at watching for color.

1.5

Designing your water system

Every life has a water loop: source → storage → purify → use → reuse → back to soil. A few truths of design hold everywhere, whatever you can change right now:

Where water falls is easiest

Places with steady rain or snow, springs and rivers need the least infrastructure. Dry places can thrive too, with storage sized for the whole dry season.

Water flows downhill

Gravity is free energy. Store high, use low; put the barrel above the garden, not below it.

Closer is easier

The shorter the trip from source to use, the less carrying, pumping and leaking.

Slow it, spread it, sink it

Rain that soaks into the ground refills wells and springs for everyone downhill.

Time decides the build

How long you’ll be somewhere, and whether you can shape it, decides whether a pitcher or a cistern makes sense.

PortableA carry-it-with-you loopFor you

Tap → filter pitcher or countertop unit → a few stored jugs → shower bucket to toilet → houseplants. Everything moves with you.

Rooted, with outdoor spaceA house-and-yard loopFor you

Tap and rain barrels → under-sink filter → stored drums → laundry-to-landscape → fruit trees and mulch. Rain gardens let what’s left soak in.

LandAn off-grid loopFor you

Well or roof catchment → cistern → sediment, carbon and UV (or slow sand) → house → gray water to orchard → swales that recharge the aquifer.

No fixed homeA map-it loopFor you

Free refill points → two or three clean bottles → a small backup filter → rinse water reused for washing up. Here the system is the map of where water is, and the people who share it.

Dry climateDesigning for the dry seasonFor you

Most of California gets its rain in a few winter months. Count how many dry months you need to bridge, and let the garden plan follow the water rather than the other way round: native, dry-adapted plants, deep mulch, and winter rain stored in the soil.

Unit 2

Sources

Where water comes from

Water reaches us from the sky, the ground or a pipe. Each source carries its own likely problems, which tells you what storage, purifying and testing it needs.

2.1

Villages grew up beside springs and rivers. People drew drinking water upstream and washed downstream, and that order still matters.

Find your watershed’s name and trace a raindrop from your roof to your tap, then to the sea.

2.2

Taps and free refills

Public water is already treated and tested, which makes it the cheapest reliable water there is. Refill maps and apps show fountains and businesses happy to fill a bottle.

State law (2012) recognizes that every person has a right to safe, clean, affordable water for drinking, cooking and washing.

No fixed homeWhere water is freeFor you

Without a home, water takes far more time, walking and planning, because most systems assume a tap of your own. That’s a gap in how things are built, not a personal failing.

  • Public libraries, parks, community and rec centers, transit stations.
  • Cafés and fast-food counters will often fill a bottle with tap water when asked.
  • Day centers and shelters often have water, showers and laundry.
  • Calling or texting 211 finds local water, showers and food.
OutageWater already in your homeFor you
  • The water heater tank, often 30–80 gallons. Turn off power or gas, let it cool, drain from the bottom valve.
  • The toilet tank (not the bowl), if no cleaning tablets are in it.
  • Ice, and liquid in canned fruit and vegetables.
  • If the supply may be contaminated, close your main valve to keep it out of your pipes.
2.3

Catching rain

One inch of rain on 1,000 sq ft of roof is about 620 gallons. A first-flush diverter sends the dirtiest first water away. Rainwater goes to the garden first, and to drinking only with treatment.

Collecting rain from your roof is legal (Rainwater Capture Act, 2012). Many water agencies offer rebates or free barrels.

PortableBalcony scaleFor you

A lidded bucket under a balcony drip or a chain from the gutter, for plants. Check with whoever owns the building before attaching anything.

YardBarrels on downspoutsFor you

One or two 55-gallon barrels with a diverter kit, a screen and an overflow pointed away from the foundation.

LandCistern catchmentFor you

Metal roofs are the best for drinking water. Gutters → first flush → screened cistern (shaded or buried) → filter train in 4.3.

Dry climateSizing for a dry seasonFor you

With winter-only rain, a barrel fills and empties within weeks. Storing water in the soil (mulch, basins, rain gardens) often beats storing it in tanks.

2.4

Wells and springs

Groundwater is filtered by soil and rock, so it’s often cleaner than surface water, but it can pick up arsenic, nitrate, uranium or bacteria along the way.

WellsCaring for a wellFor you
  • Test yearly for coliform bacteria and nitrate, and after floods or repairs (5.4).
  • Keep a sealed cap and slope soil away from the wellhead. No chemicals or septic nearby.
  • County environmental health handles well permits and drilling records.
SpringsUsing a springFor you

Find out who owns or cares for it. A “spring box” protects it from runoff. Clear spring water can still carry germs from animals uphill, so treat it unless it’s tested.

2.5

Rivers, lakes and the wild

  • Running over still: moving water is less likely to breed mosquitoes and algae.
  • But ask who’s upstream: towns, farms, roads, mines and factories all drain somewhere. Running water carries their runoff too.
  • Skip water with green or blue-green scum, oily sheen, foam or dead fish.
  • Always purify before drinking (Unit 4).

“Running water is clean water” held when nobody upstream was spraying fields. Keep the habit, and add the question.

Unit 3

Storage

Keeping water safe

Stored water stays good with three things: a clean food-grade container, darkness and coolness. Then a date on the lid, and a reminder to refresh it.

  • 3.1Containers
  • 3.2Where
  • 3.3Algae
  • 3.4Fresh
  • 3.5How much
3.1

Containers

  • Food-grade only: HDPE plastic (#2), containers sold for water, glass, stainless steel or glazed ceramic.
  • Never one that held chemicals, even rinsed. Milk jugs break down and are hard to clean: plant water only.
  • Secondhand first: food-grade barrels and jugs turn up free from bakeries, juice and soda bottlers, and on Craigslist, Facebook Marketplace and Buy Nothing. Ask what was in them.

Unglazed clay pots keep water cool: a little seeps through the walls and evaporates, carrying heat away.

More spaceDrums, totes and cisternsFor you

A 55-gallon drum full of water weighs about 460 lb: fill it where it will stay and use a hand pump or siphon. 275-gallon IBC totes work for garden water; for drinking, only food-grade ones, covered from light.

On the moveWhat to carryFor you

A gallon weighs about 8.3 lb. Two or three sturdy refillable bottles plus a collapsible pouch usually beat one big jug.

3.2

Where to keep it

Cool, dark and away from fumes. Gasoline, paint, pesticides and cleaners can pass through plastic into the water. Spread it around, so one damaged spot doesn’t take it all.

Small spacesHiding spots indoorsFor you

Under beds, the back of closets, under the sink (away from cleaners), flat bricks behind the couch.

3.3

Beware green in the sun

Algae only need light, water and a few nutrients. A clear jug on a sunny porch can turn green in weeks.

  • Opaque containers, or cover clear ones and keep them in the dark.
  • Rain barrels: opaque, lidded and screened.
  • Pet bowls and bottles by a window grow the same film in miniature.

Standing water breeds mosquitoes: eggs can become biting adults in about a week. Empty, cover or keep water moving.

Walk the house and yard with a child and find every place water sits in sunlight or stands still. Cover, empty or move each one.

Animal care · 4.4 Sunlight as a tool (the short-trip exception)

3.4

Keeping it fresh

  • Before filling: wash with soap, rinse, swish with a little unscented bleach and water (about 1 tsp per quart), then rinse.
  • Label: “filled on” date. Refresh tap water you bottled yourself every 6 months; sealed bottled water lasts to its date.
  • Pour, don’t dip: a spigot or pouring keeps hands and cups out of the supply.
  • Old stored water makes great plant water.
3.5

How much to keep

A short-term bare minimum is about 1 gallon per person per day. For longer stretches, humanitarian groups plan around 4 gallons (15 L) to cover cooking and washing. Three days is a start; two weeks gives real ease. There’s no rush: a few jugs a month adds up.

Unit 4

Purify

From a pitcher to an aquifer

No single method does everything, and the priciest one isn’t automatically right. Start with what’s in your water, then choose the simplest thing that handles it, at whatever scale your life has right now.

4.1

A filter’s box can claim anything. An independent certification (in North America, NSF/ANSI: 42 taste, 53 health contaminants like lead, 58 reverse osmosis, 401 newer chemicals) means someone outside the company tested that claim. NSF’s list is free to search by model number.

4.2

Everyday filtering

  • Free first: an open pitcher in the fridge overnight lets chlorine leave (not chloramine). Run the cold tap after water sits, cook with cold, clean the aerator.
  • Secondhand first: ask Buy Nothing groups and neighbors, then eBay, Craigslist, Facebook Marketplace and OfferUp. Used body, new insides: always fit new cartridges and sanitize the housing. Check that replacement filters are still sold.
  • Care: write the install date on every cartridge. An old filter can stop working or grow bacteria.
Can’t change pipesReverse osmosis as a stop-gapFor you

When testing shows lead, nitrate or PFAS and the pipes aren’t yours to change, a countertop reverse osmosis unit (no drilling) removes most of them and moves with you. It sends some water down the drain: catch that for mopping or flushing.

Still worth a friendly request to whoever owns the building or runs the water: fixes upstream help everyone.

Emotions & love (Request tool)

After ROPutting minerals backFor you

RO water is very low in calcium and magnesium, so it tastes flat. Most minerals come from food, so this is mostly about taste and a small top-up, and water that tastes good gets drunk.

  • A remineralizing cartridge after the membrane.
  • Trace-mineral drops, per the label.
  • A small pinch of mineral salt per pitcher, to taste.

A cheap TDS meter shows when the membrane is tiring: the number creeps up.

RootedUnder-sink and whole-houseFor you

Under-sink carbon or RO at the kitchen tap covers drinking and cooking. Whole-house sediment and carbon help with taste and shower chlorine. If lead is the concern, replacing the service line fixes the source.

4.3

Permanent systems

For places you’ll stay and can shape, purification becomes part of the land itself: a chain of steps, each catching what the last one missed.

WellA well water trainFor you

Well → pressure tank → sediment filter → carbon → UV light where water enters the house. Add a treatment for whatever testing finds (an iron filter, RO at the kitchen sink for nitrate or arsenic).

RainRain to drinking waterFor you

First flush → screened, dark cistern → floating intake (draws from just under the surface, above settled sediment) → sediment → carbon → UV. Test before trusting it.

Low-techSlow sand and biosand filtersFor you

Water trickles through fine sand; a living layer on top (the “schmutzdecke”) eats germs. Buildable from a barrel, gravel and sand, no power. Removes most germs and cloudiness, not chemicals. Takes a few weeks to mature.

Sand, charcoal, settling and sunlight are some of the oldest water tools people have.

LandFeeding the aquiferFor you

The longest-term system: help rain sink in instead of running off. Swales, rain gardens, mulch and less pavement recharge the groundwater that wells and springs draw from, for everyone downhill too.

4.4

Crisis: boil, bleach, sun

These keep people alive for days when the usual systems stop. They aren’t meant to be how we drink every day.

  1. Settle: let cloudy water sit, pour off the clearer top.
  2. Strain: through clean cloth, a coffee filter or paper towel.
  3. Boil: a full rolling boil kills germs. Many people keep it boiling for 1 minute to be sure (3 minutes at high altitude).

Boiling won’t remove fuel, chemicals, metals or algae toxins. If a source may have those, choose another one.

Boiling water into tea, soup and broth made doubtful water safe long before anyone knew about germs.

Can’t boilThe actual bleach situationFor you
What it does
  • Kills most bacteria and viruses in clear water.
  • Plain, unscented bleach only (about 6–8%). No scented, splash-less or color-safe.
  • Stir, wait 30 minutes. A slight chlorine smell means it worked. No smell? Repeat, wait 15 more.
What it doesn’t
  • Doesn’t reliably kill Cryptosporidium. Boiling does.
  • Doesn’t remove lead, nitrate, fuel or PFAS.
  • With muddy or leafy water it forms by-products. Settle and strain first.
  • Loses strength over months: date the bottle, replace yearly.
Why it’s for crises

Dosing jugs by hand every day is guesswork on strength, timing and by-products. For everyday life, tap or a matched filter is gentler and tastier.

Safety: keep bleach away from ammonia and vinegar. Mixed, they release toxic gas.

Bleach drops
FreeSunlight as a tool (SODIS)For you

Clear water in a clear plastic bottle (2 L or smaller), lying in full sun for about 6 hours (2 days if cloudy), is disinfected by UV. The flip side of 3.3: a short trip in the sun cleans; a long stay grows algae.

NoticesDuring a boil-water noticeFor you

Use boiled or bottled water for drinking, brushing teeth, ice, baby bottles and washing produce. Showering is fine; keep it out of your mouth. When it lifts, run taps for a few minutes, empty the ice maker and change fridge and pitcher filters.

Emergency preparedness · First aid · Cleaning

4.5

With no fixed home

Being unhoused makes all of this much harder: more carrying, more asking, fewer places to wash and store. These are real options, cheapest first.

  • Free: refill points (2.2), SODIS with clear bottles, rinsing bottles with hot water and soap at a library or day center.
  • A few dollars: a small dropper bottle of plain bleach (date it).
  • Around $10–40: a squeeze hollow-fiber filter for bacteria and parasites. Buy this one new (a used one can’t be checked for cracks, and boiling or freezing damages it); keep it from freezing.
  • Tablets: chlorine dioxide tablets are light and handle Cryptosporidium too, with a longer wait (check the label, up to 4 hours).
  • Heat: drink before you’re thirsty; shade and a slow pace stretch water further.

Unit 5

Testing

Knowing it’s safe

Your senses and surroundings catch a lot; the serious problems are often invisible. Testing turns worry into a clear picture, and much of it is free.

  • 5.1Safe
  • 5.2Signs
  • 5.3Report
  • 5.4Kits & labs
  • 5.5Outdoors
5.1

What “safe” means

  • Germs: bacteria, viruses, parasites. Invisible; mostly from animal or human waste. Act fast (hours to days).
  • Chemicals and metals: lead, nitrate, arsenic, pesticides, PFAS. Invisible, often tasteless; they add up over years.
  • Nuisances: chlorine taste, cloudiness, scale, smells. Easy to notice, usually harmless.

Sparkling stream water can carry Giardia; cloudy tap water after a repair is often just air bubbles that clear from the bottom up.

Turn over a few stones in a creek with a child and count the little creatures clinging to them. That’s a real field test.

Identification

5.3

Your water report

Every public water system publishes a yearly report of what it found. Search your water provider’s name + “water quality report.” Each line shows the legal limit and the health goal. Goals are set on health alone and are often stricter (for lead, the goal is zero), so compare to those.

The State Water Board publishes test results for every public system and maps lead service lines; ask your provider about yours.

Find your report and circle anything above its health goal. Bring it to 4.1.

5.4

Kits and labs

  • TDS meter (a few dollars): dissolved minerals. Good for checking an RO filter; it can’t see germs, lead or PFAS.
  • Test strips: rough reads for hardness, pH, chlorine, nitrate. Good for spotting changes.
  • Certified lab: the real answer for bacteria, lead, arsenic, PFAS. Ask your water provider or county first: many offer free or discounted kits.

Look for labs accredited by the state’s ELAP program.

WellsA yearly testing rhythmFor you

Coliform bacteria and nitrate every year; arsenic, uranium and others every few years; again after floods, repairs or a change in taste.

Older buildingsA first-draw lead testFor you

Test water that sat in the pipes overnight. Lead matters most for children and pregnancy. Share results with neighbors in the building: one test can help everyone.

5.5

Swimming and bathing outdoors

Rivers, lakes and the sea can be lovely places to wash and swim. A few checks keep it that way.

  • Skip it for 2–3 days after heavy rain, when sewage and street runoff wash in.
  • Skip water with scum, foam, oily sheen, dead fish or a chemical smell. When in doubt, stay out, and keep dogs out too.
  • Stay away from pipes, storm drains and industrial, farm or mining runoff.
  • Don’t swallow, cover cuts, rinse off afterwards.

Heal the Bay’s Beach Report Card grades the coast weekly; the state’s harmful algal bloom map covers lakes and rivers.

Warm waterHot springs and warm lakesFor you

Warm fresh water can rarely carry an amoeba (Naegleria fowleri) that is dangerous if it goes up the nose. Keep your head above water or use a nose clip. Same reason to use boiled or distilled water, never plain tap, in a neti pot.

CluesReading fish advisoriesFor you

A “don’t eat the fish” advisory points to mercury, PCBs or other chemicals in the water. Those mostly matter for eating fish, but they’re a signal to choose another spot for regular bathing and never to drink from it, even filtered.
