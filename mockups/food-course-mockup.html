Fun fact

Every food in this course comes from plants. Kinship is a vegan project, a practice of peace with animals, and plants turn out to be the cheapest, longest-keeping and most shareable way to meet the need for food. Beans and rice alone fed much of the world for thousands of years. (More on the Animals page.)

Safety numbers come from public food-safety guidance (USDA, FDA, the UK’s NHS and the National Center for Home Food Preservation). Laws mentioned are California’s; food itself is the same everywhere.

Gather

Where food comes from

Food reaches us from shops, gardens, neighbors, food banks and the wild. Knowing all the ways in means more than one strategy for the same need, so a hard week has options.

1.2

Free and shared food

Free food is a strategy like any other, and using it frees money for other needs. Food banks often have more than they can give out, especially fresh produce.

  • Calling or texting 211 finds local food banks, pantries and free meals.
  • Community fridges (“free fridges”): take what you need, leave what you can.
  • Food Not Bombs groups share free vegetarian or vegan meals in many cities.

CalFresh (California’s name for SNAP) puts money for food on a card; many farmers’ markets match it through Market Match. All public school students can get free breakfast and lunch under the state’s universal school meals program.

No fixed homeFood without a kitchenFor you

Without a home, food takes far more time and planning, because most food systems assume a kitchen and a fridge of your own. That’s a gap in how things are built, not a personal failing.

  • Shelters, day centers and churches often serve hot meals; 211 has the times.
  • Ask pantries for “no-cook” or “ready-to-eat” bags: many keep them.
  • Food that keeps without cold: nut butter, crackers, bread, whole fruit, canned beans with pull-tab lids, oats for cold soaking (4.2).
With kidsFood for childrenFor you
  • WIC helps pregnant people, babies and children under 5 with food and nutrition support.
  • Many districts run free summer meals for anyone under 18; 211 finds sites.

Emotions & love (asking for help) · Cooperatives

1.3

Stretching what you spend

  • Dried beans usually cost half or less per serving than canned, and keep for years (Unit 3).
  • Buy the staples in bulk with friends or a co-op: a 25-lb sack of rice or beans split three ways.
  • Cook once, eat three times: a big pot of beans becomes soup, tacos and a spread.
  • Frozen vegetables and fruit are picked ripe, cost less than fresh out of season, and don’t go to waste.

A shared spreadsheet with two neighbors: who’s buying the oats sack this month, who has the jars.

1.4

Growing and foraging

Even a windowsill grows herbs and sprouts. More space adds greens, beans, squash and fruit trees. Gleaning, picking what farms and backyard trees leave behind, puts fruit that would rot to use.

Only eat a wild plant you can name with certainty, from a clean place (away from roads and sprayed edges). Wild mushrooms need an expert, every time.

Picking plants in state parks is generally not allowed; rules differ on other land. Ask before picking from a tree on someone else’s property, and fallen-fruit maps show public trees.

PortableA windowsill harvestFor you

Sprouts in a jar (3.5), herbs in pots, scallions regrowing in a glass of water. Everything moves with you.

YardBeds and fruit treesFor you

Greens and beans in spring and fall, squash and tomatoes in summer. Fruit trees are a long gift: plant where you’ll stay.

Gardening · Water (gray water to trees)

LandStaple cropsFor you

Dry beans, winter squash, potatoes and grains store for months without power, so a land garden can carry the pantry, not just the salad.

1.5

Passing it on

Too many lemons, half a pot of soup, a sack that’s too big: extra food meets someone else’s need. Neighbors, Buy Nothing groups, community fridges and food banks all take it.

Since 2022, many grocery stores, distributors and large kitchens must donate edible food they’d otherwise throw away to food recovery groups (SB 1383). Federal and state law protect people who donate food in good faith.

A fruit-picking afternoon with the kids next door, then a box on the curb with a “free” sign.

Unit 2

Cleanse

Washing food well

Clean running water and a bit of rubbing do most of the work. No soap, no bleach, no special sprays.

  • 2.1Why
  • 2.2Produce
  • 2.3Greens
  • 2.4Grains & beans
  • 2.5Hands & tools
2.1

Why we wash

Washing lifts off soil, germs from the field and many hands, and some of the residue from sprays. It matters most for food eaten raw, and for anything you cut, since a knife carries what’s on the skin into the flesh.

  • Wash just before eating or cooking, not before storing: damp produce spoils faster.
  • Wash even if you’ll peel it (melons, oranges, avocados).
  • Bags labelled “pre-washed” or “triple-washed” are ready as they are; rewashing adds more chances for germs from the sink.

Water (1.2 · drinking-grade water for food eaten raw)

2.2

Fruits and vegetables

  • Firm produce (potatoes, carrots, apples, melons): rinse under running water and scrub with a clean brush.
  • Soft fruit (berries, tomatoes, peaches): a gentle rinse in a colander, then let them dry.
  • Mushrooms: a quick rinse or a damp cloth, right before cooking.
  • Dry with a clean towel: rubbing removes a little more.

For less surface residue on apples and other firm fruit: soak 12–15 minutes in 1 tsp baking soda per 2 cups of water, then rinse. A 2017 study found this beat plain water and bleach washes.

Many cooks soak produce in vinegar water. It’s pleasant and harmless, though studies find it does little more than plain water.

2.3

Leafy greens and herbs

Grit hides between leaves. Separate them, drop them into a big bowl of cold water, swish, wait a minute, then lift the greens out so the grit stays at the bottom. Repeat until the bowl is clean.

  • Toss the outer leaves of heads of lettuce and cabbage.
  • Spin or roll in a towel: dry greens last longer and hold dressing.

The swishing water is clean enough for houseplants (Water 1.2, grade 2).

Swishing and spinning is perfect work for small hands.

2.4

Grains, beans and rice

  • Dried beans and lentils: spread on a tray and pick out small stones and shrivelled beans, then rinse.
  • Quinoa: rinse well in a fine sieve. Its coating (saponins) tastes soapy and bitter.
  • Rice: rinse until the water runs mostly clear for fluffier rice.
Rice and arsenic

Rice soaks up more arsenic from soil and water than most grains, brown rice more than white. Cooking it like pasta, in plenty of water (6–10 parts water to 1 rice) and draining, removes about 40–60%. A 2020 University of Sheffield method removed about half from brown rice and nearly three-quarters from white: boil 5 minutes in fresh water, drain, then add fresh water and finish cooking with the lid on.

Variety helps too: oats, millet, quinoa, barley and buckwheat for some of rice’s meals, especially for babies and toddlers.

2.5

Hands, boards and sponges

  • Wash hands with soap for about 20 seconds before cooking and after handling soil or raw sprouts.
  • Hot soapy water for boards and knives. Deep grooves in a board hold germs: sand wood, retire scarred plastic.
  • Sponges grow germs fast. Let them dry between uses and replace often; cloths that go in the laundry stay cleaner.

Cleaning (surfaces) · Toxins (plastic boards and heat)

No sinkWashing food with little waterFor you
  • Choose food you peel: bananas, oranges, avocados, carrots you can scrape.
  • A bottle of drinking water poured over produce in a bag or bowl does the job.
  • Hand sanitizer is a backup for hands, not a wash for food.

Unit 3

Rehydrate

Waking up dried food

Dried food is food with the water taken out, so it keeps. Putting the water back, slowly and cleanly, turns a jar of hard beans into dinner.

  • 3.1Why dried
  • 3.2Beans
  • 3.3Grains & nuts
  • 3.4Fruit & mushrooms
  • 3.5Sprouting
  • ToolSoak & cook
3.1

Why dried food

Germs and molds need water to grow. Take it out and food keeps for months or years with no fridge, weighs a fraction as much, and costs less. Beans, lentils, grains, dried fruit, mushrooms and seaweed are the backbone of a pantry that doesn’t need power.

Sun-dried fruit, beans hung in their pods, strings of chilies and mushrooms on thread: drying is one of the oldest ways people have carried food through winter and across distance.

3.2

Soaking beans

  • Overnight soak: cover sorted, rinsed beans with about three times their volume of water for 8–12 hours. In a hot kitchen, soak in the fridge.
  • Quick soak: bring to a boil, boil 2–3 minutes, cover and rest 1 hour.
  • Pour off the soaking water and cook in fresh water. Soaking cuts cooking time and, for many people, gas.
  • No soak needed: lentils, split peas and mung beans cook in under an hour.
  • A spoon of salt per quart of soaking water gives softer skins. Very old beans may never fully soften; a pinch of baking soda helps.

Red kidney beans (and, less so, other beans) carry a natural toxin that a hard boil destroys. Soak, drain, then boil hard for at least 10 minutes before simmering or putting them in a slow cooker. Warm-but-not-boiling beans can be worse than raw, so never start kidney beans in a slow cooker from dry.

3.3

Grains, nuts and seeds

  • Oats: rolled oats soaked overnight in water or plant milk are ready to eat with no cooking. Steel-cut oats soaked overnight cook in about 10 minutes.
  • Brown rice and whole grains: a few hours’ soak shortens cooking and gives a softer grain. Optional.
  • Nuts: cashews soaked 2–4 hours blend into a smooth cream for sauces.
  • Chia and flax: 1 tbsp ground flax or chia in 3 tbsp water, rested 10 minutes, binds like an egg in baking.

Overnight oats in a jar, made with the kids before bed: breakfast is waiting.

3.4

Fruit, mushrooms and vegetables

  • Dried mushrooms: hot water for 20–30 minutes. Strain the soaking liquid through a cloth (grit) and keep it: it’s a rich broth.
  • Dried fruit: warm water or tea for 10–30 minutes, for baking or softening for little ones.
  • Seaweed: wakame and kombu swell a lot in 5–10 minutes. Start small.
  • Dried vegetables and TVP (textured soy protein): equal parts boiling water, 10 minutes.

Use soaked food within a few hours, or put it in the fridge. Once wet, it spoils like fresh food.

3.5

Sprouting

A jar, a mesh lid and two rinses a day turn lentils, mung beans, chickpeas or alfalfa seeds into crunchy sprouts in 2–5 days. The cheapest fresh greens there are, grown on a counter.

  1. Soak seeds overnight, drain.
  2. Tip the jar upside down at an angle so it drains.
  3. Rinse with fresh drinking water morning and evening.
  4. Eat when the tails are as long as the seed; fridge after that.

The warm, damp jar that grows sprouts also grows germs. Cooking sprouts makes them safe for young children, pregnant people, older people and anyone with a weaker immune system. Don’t sprout kidney beans to eat raw. Use seeds sold for sprouting.

Unit 4

Cook

Heat, fuel and the plate

Cooking makes food safer, easier to digest and better to share. It needs less fuel and fewer tools than most kitchens suggest.

  • 4.1Safe heat
  • 4.2With little
  • 4.3Together
  • 4.4Pots & stoves
  • 4.5The plate
4.1

Heat makes food safe

Most germs that make people sick grow fastest between 40°F and 140°F (4–60°C), the danger zone. Food is safest kept cold below it or hot above it, and passing through it quickly.

  • Reheat leftovers until steaming all the way through: 165°F (74°C) on a thermometer.
  • Soups and stews: bring back to a rolling boil.
  • Kidney beans: a hard 10-minute boil (3.2).

Stick a cheap food thermometer into tonight’s reheated leftovers. Is the middle as hot as the edge?

4.2

Cooking with little

Fuel, time and money go further with a few tricks.

  • Lids on: a covered pot boils faster and simmers on the lowest flame.
  • Pressure cookers cook soaked beans in minutes instead of an hour or two.
  • Retained heat: bring a pot to a boil, then wrap it in blankets or set it in an insulated box. It keeps cooking for hours with no fuel.

The haybox: a wooden crate packed with hay, used across Europe and Africa to finish porridges, beans and stews while the household went about its day.

One applianceHot plate, rice cooker or microwaveFor you
  • A rice cooker steams vegetables in a basket above the rice, and makes oats and lentil stew.
  • A single electric pressure cooker does beans, grains, soups and steaming.
  • Microwave: red lentils in a big bowl with water (they foam), potatoes, frozen vegetables, oats.
Shared kitchenCooking in a shared kitchenFor you

Batch-cook on quiet hours, label your shelf, and keep one crate of your own tools. A shared pot of soup is a lovely way to meet housemates.

Emotions & love (housemates) · Cleaning (shared chores)

No cookingCold soaking and no-cook mealsFor you
  • Cold soak in a jar with a tight lid: rolled oats (overnight), couscous (1–2 hours), instant noodles (about an hour), instant mashed potatoes (minutes).
  • Canned beans, chickpeas and lentils are already cooked: rinse and eat.
  • Nut butter, bread, fruit and canned beans meet protein, energy and fiber with no heat.
OutageCooking without powerFor you
  • Camp stoves and grills are outdoor tools only: their fumes can kill indoors, even with a window open.
  • Eat from the fridge first, then the freezer, then the pantry (5.1).
  • A pot brought to a boil on a camp stove and wrapped in blankets saves fuel.

Air (carbon monoxide) · Emergency preparedness

OutdoorsSun and fireFor you

A solar oven (a box, a black pot, foil and glass) slow-cooks beans and rice on sunny days. Rocket stoves burn small sticks hot and clean.

Food left wrapped for many hours can cool into the danger zone. If a retained-heat pot has sat more than about 4 hours, bring it back to a boil before eating.

4.3

Cooking together

Children belong in the kitchen, at whatever level they can manage. Real tools, real jobs, next to the adults.

ToddlersWash and carry

Swishing greens, scrubbing potatoes, tearing lettuce, carrying the bowl to the table.

PreschoolPour and mash

Measuring beans into a jar, mashing chickpeas, stirring cold batters, sorting stones from lentils.

Early schoolCut and mix

A small sharp knife on soft food with a claw grip, cracking open pods, rinsing quinoa.

Older kidsCook a dish

The stove with an adult nearby, then a whole meal of their own for everyone.

Sorting a tray of dried beans for stones is a favorite: it’s a treasure hunt that makes dinner.

4.4

Pots, pans and stoves

  • Cast iron, carbon steel, stainless steel and glass last a lifetime and turn up secondhand.
  • Nonstick coatings: keep the heat low and retire pans once scratched or flaking. See Toxins for what’s in them.
  • Gas stoves put fumes into the room. Run the hood fan or open a window while cooking.
  • Hot food goes into glass, steel or ceramic rather than plastic.

Toxins (cookware, plastics) · Air (gas stoves, ventilation)

4.5

A plate that meets needs

Bodies need energy, protein, fats, fiber, vitamins and minerals. A plant plate meets nearly all of them with variety across the day. Major dietetic associations find well-planned vegan diets healthy at every stage of life, including pregnancy and childhood.

Beans and lentils

Protein, iron, fiber. Tofu, tempeh, peanuts count too.

Whole grains

Energy and more protein: rice, oats, wheat, millet, corn.

Vegetables and fruit

Vitamins, fiber; vitamin C helps the body take in iron.

Nuts and seeds

Fats; ground flax, chia and walnuts for omega-3.

A few to plan for
  • B12: made by bacteria, not plants. A supplement or fortified foods (plant milks, nutritional yeast) are the reliable source. Many older adults need one whatever they eat.
  • Calcium: fortified plant milk, calcium-set tofu, kale, bok choy.
  • Vitamin D: sun and fortified foods. Iodine: iodized salt or seaweed in small amounts.

A doctor or dietitian can check levels and doses, especially in pregnancy and for young children.

Look at one day’s plates. Which of the four groups showed up? Which one would be easy to add tomorrow?

Unit 5

Store

Keeping food good

Every way of keeping food takes away something germs need: warmth, water, air or a friendly pH. Cold, dry, sealed and sour are the four big tools.

  • 5.1Cold
  • 5.2Pantry
  • 5.3Leftovers
  • 5.4Longer
  • 5.5Visitors
  • ToolsKeeps & outage
5.1

Cold

  • Fridge at 40°F (4°C) or below, freezer at 0°F (−18°C). A $5 thermometer tells you.
  • Greens in a container lined with a dry towel. Herbs like basil and cilantro in a glass of water, like flowers.
  • Apples, bananas, avocados and tomatoes give off ethylene, a ripening gas: keep them away from greens and carrots.
  • Tomatoes, potatoes, onions, garlic, winter squash and bananas keep better out of the fridge.
No fridgeLiving without coldFor you
  • Buy fresh food for a day or two at a time; lean on the pantry (5.2) for the rest.
  • Whole fruit and hard vegetables keep for days in shade: apples, citrus, carrots, cabbage, potatoes, onions, squash.
  • A clay pot-in-pot cooler (a pot inside a bigger pot, wet sand between) cools by evaporation in dry heat.
  • Cook only what gets eaten in one go.
Small fridgeMaking a small fridge go furtherFor you

Save cold space for what needs it: cooked food, cut fruit, tofu, opened plant milk, greens. Everything in the list above that keeps better outside can live on the counter.

OutageWhen the power goes outFor you
  • Keep the doors shut. A closed fridge stays cold about 4 hours; a full freezer about 48 hours, a half-full one about 24.
  • Use the outage tool below to sort what’s safe.
  • Frozen jugs of water (Water 3.5) keep a freezer cold longer, and become drinking water.
5.2

The pantry

Cool, dark, dry and sealed. Glass jars with good lids keep out air, damp and visitors (5.5).

  • Keep for years: white rice, dried beans, salt, sugar, dried pasta. Old beans are still safe, they just cook slower.
  • Keep for months: brown rice, whole-wheat flour, nuts and seeds. Their oils go rancid (a smell like crayons or old paint); the fridge or freezer doubles their life.
  • First in, first out: new jars behind old ones, and a date on the lid.

Since July 2026, “BEST if Used by” means quality and “USE by” means safety on food labels (AB 660). “Best by” food past its date is usually fine: look, smell, taste a little.

Take everything off one shelf. Eat the oldest thing this week.

5.3

Leftovers

  • Into the fridge within 2 hours of cooking (1 hour on a day over 90°F).
  • Shallow containers cool fast. A big pot of soup can stay warm in the middle for hours: split it up.
  • Most leftovers keep 3–4 days cold, or months frozen. Freeze in single portions with a date.

Cooked rice can carry spores that survive cooking and make a toxin reheating won’t destroy. Cool it within an hour, keep it cold, and eat it within a day (the UK’s NHS advice; US guidance allows 3–4 days). Reheat only once. Your nose can’t tell you about this one.

5.4

Keeping it longer

EasyFreezingFor you

Blanch most vegetables 1–3 minutes first so they keep color and taste. Freeze fruit on a tray, then bag it. Bread, cooked beans, grains and soup all freeze well.

EasyFermentingFor you

Sauerkraut and kimchi: shred vegetables, mix with 2% salt by weight (20 g per kg), pack into a jar and keep them under the brine. Ready in 1–4 weeks at room temperature.

A flat white film (kahm yeast) can be skimmed. Fuzzy blue, green, black or pink mold means the batch goes to compost.

Some gearDryingFor you

Sun, an oven at its lowest setting with the door ajar, or a dehydrator. Fruit is done when leathery with no wet pockets, vegetables when brittle.

Some gearCanningFor you
  • High-acid food (most fruit, pickles, tomatoes with added lemon juice) goes in a boiling water bath.
  • Low-acid food (beans, most vegetables, soups) needs a pressure canner. A water bath isn’t hot enough to kill botulism spores.
  • Use tested recipes (the National Center for Home Food Preservation or the USDA guide) and adjust for altitude.
LandRoot cellars and cool roomsFor you

Potatoes, carrots, beets, cabbage, apples and winter squash keep for months somewhere cold (32–40°F), dark and damp. A buried bin, a north-side closet or an unheated garage can work.

Bulging, leaking or spurting cans and jars, or an off smell: throw out without tasting, in a sealed bag. Garlic or herbs in oil: keep in the fridge and use within 4 days, or freeze.

Every culture has its keeping foods: kimchi, sauerkraut, miso, pickled plums, dried tomatoes, fruit leather. Each one turns a glut into a year of meals.

5.5

Animals in the pantry

Moths in the flour, ants on the counter, a mouse behind the rice: they’re neighbors whose need for food our pantry is meeting by accident. Change what we offer and they move on, no poison needed.

  • Pantry moths and weevils: freeze new flour and grain for about four days, then store in glass or metal. Compost what they’ve moved into, wipe the shelf.
  • Ants: follow the trail to the door, wipe it with soapy water or vinegar (it erases their scent path), and seal the crack.
  • Mice: food in jars and tins, crumbs swept, and gaps around pipes stuffed with steel wool. Live traps if someone’s already living in.

Bay leaves in the flour bin are a common old remedy for weevils. Studies are thin, but they smell nice and do no harm.
