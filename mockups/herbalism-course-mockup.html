How sure are we?

Every use on an herb page carries one of these four tags, so a cup of tea for a sore stomach and a claim about memory never look equally certain.

TestedGood trials

Several well-run human trials agree. Still worth checking with a clinician if you take medicines.

SomeSmall studies

A few small or short trials point the same way. Promising, not settled.

TraditionalLong use

Used safely for generations, and recognised by regulators for mild problems, without strong trials.

UnknownNot yet known

Too little research, or results that disagree. Fine to enjoy, unwise to rely on.

Fun fact

Rosemary is so good at slowing fats from going stale that its extract is added to cooking oils and snacks as a natural preservative. The plant that keeps your oil fresh is the same one you just cut off the hedge.

Doses on the herb pages come from the European Medicines Agency’s herbal monographs, which list traditional uses, amounts and cautions for each plant. Drying times come from university extension guides. Animal safety comes from the ASPCA’s plant lists.

Rosemary

R.1

Meet rosemary

A woody Mediterranean shrub with needle-like leaves, green on top and silvery underneath, and small blue to purple flowers. Every kind sold as rosemary (upright, trailing, ‘Tuscan Blue’, ‘Arp’) is the same species, and all of it is edible.

  • What it needs: full sun, soil that drains fast, and very little water once settled. Wet roots are its main trouble.
  • After a hard trim: rosemary regrows from green, leafy stems, but rarely from bare brown wood. If whole branches were cut back to bare wood, those may stay bare. A deep watering now helps the rest push new growth before winter.
  • Who else lives here? Rosemary is on the ASPCA’s non-toxic list for cats, dogs and horses, so the drying rack can sit where they roam. Rosemary essential oil is a different matter (R.7).

Rosemary is made for California: drought-tolerant, loved by bees in the winter months when little else flowers, and happy on almost no summer water.

“There’s rosemary, that’s for remembrance.” Rosemary was tucked into wedding wreaths and laid on graves across Europe as a sign of memory. Modern research has started to test that link (R.7).

R.2

Sort the pile (today)

Cut herbs start to wilt and heat up in a heap within a day, so sorting comes first. Tip the pile onto a tarp or old sheet in the shade and make three piles.

Kitchen & medicine

Green, leafy tips and soft stems, roughly the top 4–6 in (10–15 cm). No brown leaves, no dust. This is the treasure.

Craft & fire

Straight woody stems thicker than a pencil: strip them for skewers, bundle them as kindling, or simmer for a hair rinse (R.8).

Compost

Brown or dusty leaves, flowers gone over, anything that smells musty, and grass or leaf litter swept up with it.

  • Ask the gardeners one question: was anything sprayed on or near these bushes? If yes, or if the bush sits by a road or where dogs mark it, that pile goes to craft and compost, not food. (More in Poisons → Pesticides.)
  • Small visitors: spittlebug foam, spiders and beetles ride in on trimmings. Shake each armful over the garden bed so they can walk home.
  • Too much? Bundles of fresh rosemary are a lovely thing to give away. A box on the curb, a local Buy Nothing group, or the neighbors who cook.

Sorting is perfect work for small hands: the green pile and the brown pile, plus a smell test for each. Children learn the herb by touch and smell long before they learn its name.

Start with the kitchen pile. If time runs out today, those are the stems worth saving first.

R.3

Clean

Garden trimmings carry dust and the odd insect, so a quick rinse helps. The goal is clean and dry, because water left on the leaves turns into mold on the drying rack.

  1. Fill a sink or big bowl with cool water. Swish a handful of sprigs, then lift them out so the grit stays at the bottom.
  2. Change the water when it looks cloudy. Twice through is usually enough.
  3. Shake hard, spin in a salad spinner if you have one, or roll in a towel.
  4. Spread on a towel in the shade or indoors until no water shows on the leaves, usually 1–3 hours. Then dry or store.

Leaves that look clean and came from a high branch away from paths can skip the rinse. Rosemary’s oils sit on the leaf surface, so less handling keeps more scent.

R.4

Dry

Rosemary is one of the easiest herbs to dry: thin leaves, little water, sturdy stems. Air drying keeps the most flavor and costs nothing.

WayHowTimeGood for
Hang in bundles5–10 sprigs per bundle, a rubber band at the stems (it tightens as they shrink), upside down somewhere warm, dark and airy. A paper bag with holes over each bundle catches falling leaves and dust.1–2 weeksBig piles, best flavor, free
Flat on a rackOne layer on a window screen, cooling rack or basket. Turn every day or two.1–2 weeksShort tips and loose leaves
Dehydrator95–115°F (35–46°C), one layer.1–4 hoursHumid weather, speed
OvenLowest setting with the door propped open, or the oven light on overnight. Rosemary loses more scent this way.2–4 hoursWhen nothing else is free
MicrowaveOne layer between paper towels, 30-second bursts.2–3 minA handful, today

Air drying works best above about 85°F (29°C) and below 60% humidity. Keep bundles away from the stove, kettle and sink. Times and temperatures from the University of Minnesota Extension.

  • Dry enough: the needles snap instead of bend, and fall off when you run a finger down the stem.
  • Strip: hold the stem tip, run your fingers down toward the cut end over a tray. Keep the needles whole; crush them only when you cook, so the oils stay locked in.
  • The jar test: fill a jar, close it, and check after 2–3 days. Fog on the glass means more drying time.

Hang three bundles today and label them with the date on a strip of masking tape.

FormHowKeeps

Frozen sprigsLaid on a tray to freeze, then packed into a bag or boxAbout 1 year; use straight into cooking

Fresh rosemary in oil is a botulism risk at room temperature, and you can’t smell or taste it. Keep fresh herb-in-oil in the fridge and use it within 4 days, or freeze it. Infused oils for the skin start from dried rosemary (R.8).

R.6

In the kitchen

Rosemary loves olive oil, garlic, lemon, beans, potatoes and bread. It is strong, so a little goes far: 1 teaspoon dried equals about 1 tablespoon fresh.

Rosemary salt

Blitz 1 part fresh leaves with 4 parts coarse salt, spread on a tray for a day or two until dry. Keeps a year. A good gift.

Roast anything

Potatoes, squash, chickpeas, carrots: oil, salt, a few chopped sprigs, hot oven.

White beans

Simmer cooked white beans with garlic, a sprig and olive oil. Mash half for toast.

Focaccia

Press whole needles and flaky salt into the dough before baking.

Rosemary syrup

Simmer 1 cup sugar, 1 cup water and 3 sprigs for 5 minutes; strain. For lemonade or tea. Fridge, about 2 weeks.

Skewers

Stripped woody stems, sharpened at one end, for grilled vegetables and tofu. Soak them first.

Pulling needles off sprigs is a job a three-year-old can do right next to you, and the smell stays on their hands all afternoon.

Make one jar of rosemary salt from today’s pile.

R.7

As medicine

Rosemary has a long record of easing a heavy stomach and sore muscles, and a few small studies on hair and memory. Here is each use with how sure we are.

UseHowAmountHow sure
Bloating, a heavy or crampy stomachTea: pour boiling water over the leaves, cover, steep 10 min1–2 g dried leaf (1–1½ tsp) in 150–250 ml water, 2–3 times a dayTraditional
Sore muscles and joints, cold hands and feetBath: simmer leaves in 1 liter of water, strain into a warm bath, soak 10–20 min50 g dried leaf per bath (a scant cup), 2–3 times a weekTraditional
Thinning hairDiluted rosemary oil massaged into the scalpDaily for months; one trial ran 6 monthsSmall studies
Focus and memorySmelling rosemary while working; teaNo set amountSmall studies
Colds, “boosting immunity”, blood sugarVarious—Not yet known

Tea and bath amounts are the European Medicines Agency’s traditional-use doses for rosemary leaf. If a problem lasts past 2 weeks (tea) or 4 weeks (bath), that’s a good time to see a clinician. The hair trial compared rosemary oil with 2% minoxidil in 100 people; the memory studies are small.

Food amounts are fine for everyone

The cautions below are about medicinal amounts: daily teas, baths and oils. A sprig in the soup is just cooking.

Talk with a clinician first if…

You’re pregnant or breastfeeding, it’s for a child under 12, or you have gallstones, a liver or bile-duct condition, high blood pressure (before hot herbal baths), or epilepsy (rosemary essential oil contains camphor).

Rosemary essential oil is far stronger than the leaf. It isn’t swallowed, it’s always diluted before touching skin, and it stays off broken or irritated skin. Diffusers are best kept away from cats, birds and small animals, whose bodies clear oils slowly.

R.8

Make: oil, salve, rinse

Rosemary infused oil Skin, scalp, massage

Use fully dried rosemary so no water gets into the oil.

  1. Fill a clean jar about half full with dried leaves; cover with olive, sunflower or jojoba oil by an inch.
  2. Slow: lid on, sunny windowsill, shake daily for 4–6 weeks. Fast: jar in a pan of barely simmering water, 2–4 hours, never boiling.
  3. Strain through cloth, squeeze, bottle, label with the date.

Keeps 6–12 months somewhere cool and dark. A bit of the rosemary keeps the oil from going stale, too.

Muscle salve From the infused oil

Melt 1 part candelilla wax into 8 parts rosemary oil by weight, gently. Drop a spoonful on a cold plate: too soft, add wax; too hard, add oil. Pour into tins.

Hair rinse Uses the woody stems

Simmer a big handful of stems and leaves in 4 cups of water for 20 minutes, cool, strain. Pour over hair after washing. Fridge, about a week.

Rosemary vinegar Cooking or cleaning

Pack dried sprigs into a jar, cover with vinegar, steep 2 weeks, strain. Apple cider vinegar for salads; plain white vinegar, diluted half and half with water, for wiping counters and glass.

R.9

Harvest next time

A light, regular harvest keeps rosemary bushy and gives you fresh sprigs all year, with fewer giant piles.

  • Cut soft, green tips 2–4 in (5–10 cm) long, just above a pair of leaves.
  • Take up to a third of the plant at a time, and stay in the leafy part.
  • Mid-morning, after the dew dries, is when the leaves hold the most oil.
  • The biggest harvest for drying: late spring or early summer, just before or as it flowers.

If you share a garden with a gardening crew, a note asking for the trimmings to be left in a pile turns their visit into your harvest day.

R.10

Grow more from scraps

Some of today’s tips can become new plants for free.

  1. Pick soft green tips 4–6 in (10–15 cm) long.
  2. Strip the leaves from the bottom half.
  3. Push them into a pot of damp sand or seed mix, or stand them in a glass of water on a bright windowsill.
  4. Roots in 3–8 weeks. Pot them up and give them away, or start a hedge of your own.

Lavender

L.1

Which lavender is it?

Unlike rosemary, “lavender” covers several plants that taste and act differently. Which one you have decides whether it goes in the cake or the sock drawer.

KindHow to spot itScentBest for
English lavender
L. angustifolia		Compact bush, short stems, small tight flower heads; ‘Hidcote’ and ‘Munstead’ are commonSweet, softFood, tea, sleep, everything
Lavandin
L. × intermedia		Big bush, long stems (often 18 in / 45 cm+), long pointed heads; ‘Grosso’, ‘Provence’Strong, sharper, a little camphorSachets, cleaning, bath; food only lightly
Spanish lavender
L. stoechas		Fat heads topped with purple “rabbit ears”Piney, camphorScent and wreaths, not food or tea

Not sure? Rub a flower and smell it. Sweet and soft is probably English; sharp and medicinal leans lavandin or Spanish. When in doubt, use it for scent, not food.

Who else lives here? The ASPCA lists lavender as toxic to cats, dogs and horses: nibbling the plant can upset their stomachs. Lavender essential oil is much stronger and especially hard on cats, so diffusers and sprays stay out of rooms where cats sleep and eat.

L.2

Sort the pile (today)

An autumn trim is mostly spent flower stalks and leafy tips. The buds hold less scent than a summer harvest, but still plenty for sachets, baths and oil.

Buds

Any stalk still holding color and scent. Bundle these to dry; the buds get rubbed off later.

Leafy tips

Gray-green leaves smell of lavender too. Dry them for sachets, bath bags and fire starters.

Bare stalks

Bundled tight with string, they make scented kindling or fire starters. Long ones weave into small wreaths.

Compost

Brown, moldy or dusty bits, and sweepings.

Bundling stalks with string is a calm job to share at a table, and the room smells wonderful afterwards. Bees often linger on lavender, so shake armfuls gently over the bed first and let them fly off.

L.3

Clean

Lavender is usually best left unwashed: the buds hold water and can mold, and most of it won’t be eaten. Shake bundles hard, outdoors, to loosen dust and insects.

For the small amount going into food or tea, rinse quickly in cool water, shake, and dry on a towel until no water shows before bundling, about 2–3 hours.

L.4

Dry

  1. Make small bundles, about as thick as a thumb, and wrap a rubber band around the stems.
  2. Hang them upside down somewhere dark, dry and airy. Darkness keeps the purple color.
  3. Or lay stalks in one layer on a screen or in a shallow basket.
  4. Dry in 1–2 weeks: stems snap, and buds fall off when rubbed. A dehydrator at 95–115°F (35–46°C) takes 2–4 hours.
  5. To collect buds, roll the dry heads between your palms over a tray, then shake through a colander or sieve to drop out bits of stem.

Hang a bundle in a closet. It dries there and scents the clothes at the same time.

L.5

Store

Dried buds go into a glass jar with a tight lid, in the dark. Scent stays strong for about a year. Label kitchen lavender separately from scent-only lavender so they don’t mix.

To wake up old buds in a sachet, squeeze it. Crushing releases a fresh round of oil.

L.6

In the kitchen

English lavender tastes floral and a little like rosemary; too much tastes like soap. Start with ¼–½ teaspoon of dried buds for a whole recipe, ground or steeped.

Lavender sugar

Pulse 1 tsp dried buds with 1 cup sugar. Sift out the bits if you like. For shortbread, tea and lemonade.

Lavender lemonade

Steep 1 tsp buds in 1 cup boiling water for 10 min, strain, sweeten, add lemon juice and cold water.

Shortbread

Plant butter, flour, lavender sugar. A classic, and very forgiving.

Herbes de Provence

Mix dried thyme, savory, rosemary, oregano and a pinch of lavender for roasted vegetables.

L.7

As medicine

Lavender is best known for calm and sleep, and it has the strongest evidence of the two herbs, though for a specific capsule rather than garden lavender.

UseHowAmountHow sure
Mild stress, tiredness, trouble falling asleepTea: boiling water over the buds, cover, steep 5–10 min1–2 g dried flower (about 1 tbsp) in 150 ml water, up to 3 times a dayTraditional
Ongoing anxietyLavender oil capsules made for medicine (Silexan), from a clinician or pharmacy, not homemade80 mg a day in trialsGood trials
Sleep and calm through scentSachet by the pillow, bath, diffuserNo set amountSmall studies
Small burns, bites, headachesVarious—Not yet known

Tea amount is the European Medicines Agency’s traditional-use dose for lavender flower. If stress or sleep troubles last or get worse, that’s a good time to see a clinician.

Lavender can make you drowsy

Go easy before driving or using machines, and check with a clinician if you take sleep, anxiety or seizure medicines, since effects can add up.

Talk with a clinician first if…

You’re pregnant or breastfeeding, or it’s a daily tea for a child under 12. Medicinal safety isn’t established there; a sachet by the bed is a gentler choice.

Lavender essential oil isn’t swallowed, and it’s diluted before it touches skin (about 1–2 drops per teaspoon of oil). A few reports have linked regular use of lavender and tea tree oil products in young boys to breast growth that went away when use stopped; the link is debated, but it’s a reason to keep oils off children day after day.

L.8

Make: sachets, spray, bath

Sachets Drawers, closets, pillows

Fill small cloth bags or tied squares of old cotton with buds and dried leaves. Clothes moths dislike the smell, so sachets help as one part of keeping wool safe, with regular airing and brushing. Squeeze to refresh.

Cleaning → Laundry

Sleep pillow A gift for a tired friend

A small cloth pouch, half buds and half dried rosemary or plain rice, tucked inside the pillowcase.

Linen spray Sheets, curtains, a stuffy room

Steep 2 tbsp buds in ½ cup boiling water, covered, 20 min; strain. Add ½ cup vodka or witch hazel so it keeps. Spray bottle, fridge, about 1 month. Spray away from cats.

Bath bag With rosemary for sore muscles

Tie a handful of buds (and rosemary, R.7) in a cloth, hang it under the hot tap, then let it float. No bits in the drain.

Fire starters Uses the bare stalks

Tight bundles of dry stalks, tied with cotton string. They catch fast and smell lovely in a fireplace or wood stove.

L.9

Harvest next time

  • For drying and scent: cut when about a third to half of the buds on a stalk have opened, usually early summer. Scent is strongest then.
  • Cut the stalk just above the leaves, mid-morning after the dew dries.
  • To keep the bush young: after flowering, shape it back by about a third, staying in the green, leafy growth. Like rosemary, lavender rarely regrows from bare wood.

In mild California climates many lavenders flower more than once. A light cut after the first bloom often brings a second, smaller one.

L.10

Grow more from scraps

Non-flowering side shoots 3–4 in (8–10 cm) long root well in damp sand or seed mix. Strip the bottom leaves, keep them bright but out of hot sun, and look for roots in 4–8 weeks. Cuttings from your own bush grow into the same kind of lavender.
